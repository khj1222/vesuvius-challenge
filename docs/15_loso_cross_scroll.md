# Measuring cross-scroll generalisation: leave-Paris4-out (2026-08-23)

Step 2 of the September track, and **the first systematic numbers for open
problem #7, "cross-scroll ink generalization"**. The public ink_9um recipe was
retrained unchanged except that the eight PHercParis4 representations were
removed (seeds 42 and 43, 78,125 steps each), and the resulting models were
scored on the whole annotation (supervision mask) of all eight Paris4 segments
as honest held-out. The reference arm is the released checkpoint — a model that
**did** train on Paris4, so the same pixels are training data for it.

## Method

- **LOSO arm**: generated by `tools/make_ink9um_config.py --exclude-scroll Paris4`
  ([configs/ink9um_loso_noParis4_s42.json](../configs/ink9um_loso_noParis4_s42.json),
  s43 identical). 21 representations (0139 x14, 1667 x6, 0814 x1), batch quota
  renormalised to {0139: 35, 1667: 27, 0814: 2}. Measured training time
  s42 2h54m / s43 3h14m on an RTX 5090 (~7-8 it/s).
- **Scoring**: a full threshold sweep per prediction, reported as best F1.
  4 arms x 7 steps (10k...75k) x 8 segments = **224 cells**; raw numbers in
  `runs/ink9um_scorecard/paris4_matrix.csv`, aggregate in
  `paris4_matrix_summary.json`.
- **Trivial floor**: for a segment with ink fraction p, the "everything is ink"
  classifier scores F1 = 2p/(1+p). A cross-scroll score means nothing except as
  **how far above that floor** it sits — and indeed some (arm, step) cells pick
  best threshold 0, which is that trivial classifier.

## What it says

1. **The size of the cross-scroll gap.** Honest against honest: the released
   model scores 0.74-0.77 on its own scroll's validation mask (docs/14), and the
   same recipe with Paris4 removed scores best-of-grid **0.49** on Paris4 —
   about **-0.26**. Against training pixels it is 0.89 vs 0.49 = -0.41.
2. **The margin over the trivial floor averages +0.06.** Most of the spread
   between segments (0.40-0.60) is explained by their ink fractions, and the
   real transfer signal is thin. It is not zero, though: w09 (+0.156) and
   w07 (+0.077) carry signal, while w03 (+0.023) and w06 (+0.027) sit at noise
   level. That the transfer signal itself swings three- to sevenfold between
   segments is the second finding.
3. **This is structure, not seed.** Mean absolute seed difference within the
   LOSO arm is **0.011** (max 0.057) — against seed gaps of up to 0.22 on
   in-scroll held-out (docs/14). Weak cross-scroll performance is not a
   lottery; it reproduces.
4. **75k overtrains here too.** LOSO peaks at 20k and loses 0.036 on average by
   75k. Only in-scroll memorisation keeps rising; no held-out axis justifies 75k.
5. **The retraining is sound.** Online validation during the LOSO runs (the
   three official masks, on scrolls that stayed in) sits at balanced accuracy
   0.69-0.76, the same band as the released runs' held-out — a control against
   "our retrain was simply weaker".

## Caveats

- Paris4 may be the hardest possible holdout: its labels are transferred onto a
  2.4 um volume (the aligned family), and even the reference model stalls at
  0.91 on training pixels where other scrolls reach 0.98+. Whether the gap is
  Paris4-specific needs **leave-1667-out and leave-0139-out arms** — one config
  line each with this harness. (Both were run; see parts 2 and 3.)
- Both sides are compared best-of-grid, which flatters both (a checkpoint
  selection oracle). Fixed-step comparisons can be rebuilt from the raw CSV.
- Scoring includes a threshold oracle (a per-image sweep). A real deployment
  additionally needs threshold transfer.

## What this implies for First Letters

Direct inference on an unseen scroll (PHerc0800/1447) carries a signal of only
**+0.02 to +0.16 over the trivial baseline**, so expecting readable letters from
it is not reasonable. The plan cannot be "render, then infer"; it has to be
"render, then **adapt to the domain** (unlabelled fine-tuning on the target
scroll, say), then infer". This document is the quantitative case for that
necessity. The render path itself is docs/13 section 6, and was later run end to
end in [docs/16](16_first_letters_render.md).

## Reproduce

```bash
# 1) arm config (the paired seed is --seed 43)
python tools/make_ink9um_config.py --exclude-scroll Paris4 \
  --out configs/ink9um_loso_noParis4_s42.json --run-dir runs/ink9um_loso_noParis4_s42
# 2) train -- cwd is the villa **tip** tree, the venv is external/villa's.
#    Running from the external/villa checkout finds zero segments: it predates
#    the ink_9um config schema.
cd D:/vw2/ink-detection
uv run --project D:/vesuvius-challenge/external/villa/ink-detection --no-sync python -m koine_machines.training.train D:/vesuvius-challenge/configs/ink9um_loso_noParis4_s42.json
# 3) score (the whole segment is held out, so score the supervision regions)
python tools/eval_validation.py <pred.tif> <labels>/phercparis4-w00 \
  --region-kind supervision_mask --no-image-metrics
```

1. **The cross-scroll gap is universal.** On both scrolls LOSO sits -0.40 to
   -0.43 below the reference, and well below the scroll's own honest ceiling
   (Paris4 about -0.26, 1667 about -0.17). The structural findings — seed
   independence, an early peak, 75k overtraining — reproduce on both.
2. **Its size depends on the target by more than a factor of two.** The transfer
   margin is +0.131 into 1667 and +0.060 into Paris4. 1667's worst segment
   (+0.092) beats every Paris4 segment except w09 (+0.156). Part 1's caveat —
   that Paris4 is an unusually hard transfer target, with transferred labels and
   a reference that itself stalls at 0.89 on training pixels — is confirmed.
3. **The summary number for open problem #7**: at 9 um, the per-pixel signal
   that survives a scroll boundary is **+0.06 (into Paris4) to +0.13 (into
   1667)** over the trivial baseline. The distance from there to a readable
   level (0.74+ within a scroll) is the quantitative target domain adaptation
   has to close.
4. Direction caveat: the two arms differ in training corpus as well (one
   contains 1667, the other Paris4), so "target difficulty" and "source
   composition" are entangled. Separating them calls for a 0139-out arm — the
   largest possible deletion, 14 representations — though that leaves only half
   the corpus and changes the reading again. Two arms are already enough for the
   universality claim.

# Part 3: leave-0139-out (2026-08-24) — the difficulty spectrum, and a representation effect

The third arm, and the most aggressive ablation: **half the corpus** — 0139's 14
representations, 9 aligned plus 5 native — removed, leaving 15 representations
(quota {1667: 40, Paris4: 20, 0814: 4}). 0139 is the only scroll whose physical
segments exist in both an aligned and a native representation, so held-out
scoring here doubles as a controlled comparison of preprocessing families. 392
cells (4 arms x 7 steps x 14 representations); raw numbers in
`runs/ink9um_scorecard/no0139_matrix.csv`, aggregate in
`no0139_matrix_summary.json`.

## Results (best-of-grid)

| block | LOSO best, mean | over floor | ref best, mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| aligned, 9 segments | 0.691 | **+0.201** | 0.982 |
| native, 5 segments | 0.653 | +0.112 | 0.980 |
| all 14 representations | **0.678** | **+0.169** | 0.981 |

Per-segment margins run from +0.040 (w040 native) to **+0.337 (w029)**; the best
single LOSO value is w043 at **0.762**, inside the released model's own
in-scroll honest band of 0.74-0.77. LOSO peaks at 20k by step (0.654), and the
mean absolute seed difference is 0.032.

## Same physical segment, two representations — aligned wins 4 out of 4

| seg | aligned (margin) | native (margin) | delta margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| w035 | 0.740 (+0.260) | 0.679 (+0.203) | +0.057 |
| w039 | 0.654 (+0.169) | 0.585 (+0.103) | +0.066 |
| w040 | 0.703 (+0.088) | 0.652 (+0.040) | +0.048 |
| w041 | 0.734 (+0.145) | 0.703 (+0.117) | +0.028 |

The measured fact goes exactly this far: **the aligned representation scores
consistently better than the native one**, by +0.03 to +0.07.

WARNING — **the first version of this document read that wrongly.** Because the
no0139 training corpus is entirely aligned (2.4 um transferred and pooled), the
result was read as **domain match** — transfer is better into the family you
trained on — and the guidance issued was "when inferring on an unseen scroll,
render in the same family you trained on". **A follow-up arm on 2026-08-26
rejected that reading** (appendix 2). A model that had seen the native family in
training showed the same gap, so the cause is not family *match* but **the
aligned representation being better on its own**.

The guidance survives in a different shape: not "render in the family you
trained on" but **"render in the aligned family (high-resolution transfer plus z
pooling)"** — whatever the training corpus was.

## The three-arm spectrum (summary table for open problem #7)

| held-out scroll | source corpus | LOSO best, mean | **margin over floor** | ref - LOSO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Paris4** (8 segments) | 21 reps | 0.487 | **+0.060** | 0.405 |
| **1667** (6 segments) | 23 reps | 0.546 | **+0.131** | 0.429 |
| **0139** (14 reps) | **15 reps (smallest)** | 0.678 | **+0.169** | 0.304 |

1. **The gap appears in 3 of 3, and the target dominates its size** — a margin
   spectrum of +0.06 to +0.13 to +0.17. Note that no0139 transfers best **while
   training on half the corpus**: the deciding variable is not source size but
   **the nature of the target scroll**. 0139 is the corpus's central scroll, so
   its scan and annotation quality are representative; Paris4 has transferred
   labels and a reference that stalls at 0.89, making it the outlier target.
2. **All three structural findings reproduce**: LOSO peaks at 10-20k and 75k
   overtrains in every arm; seeds are near-irrelevant (mean absolute difference
   0.011 / 0.015 / 0.032, against 0.22 in-scroll); and the margin varies widely
   between segments.
3. **How far from readable**: even in the best case (0139) the mean is 0.68, and
   the remaining distance to the in-scroll honest ceiling (0.74+) is domain
   adaptation's share. That individual segments — w029, w035, w041, w043 —
   already reach that band is the encouraging part.

# Part 4: the nature of the gap, and what it costs to close (2026-08-24)

Three arms measured the gap's existence, size and structure. The last question
is how to close it. Two methods, measured on the same harness: the free one
(ensembling) and the cheapest paid one (fine-tuning on one target segment).

## 4a. Ensembling recovers only +0.005 to +0.009

Built by reusing the existing prediction TIFFs, so no GPU: a **seed ensemble**
(mean of s42 and s43, per step) and a **step ensemble** (mean of 10k, 20k and
30k, per seed). 252 cells; raw numbers in
`runs/ink9um_scorecard/loso_ensembles.csv` (plus `_summary.json`).

| arm | single best | seed ensemble | gain | step-ensemble gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| into Paris4 | 0.487 | 0.493 | +0.005 | -0.004 |
| into 1667 | 0.546 | 0.555 | +0.009 | +0.007 |
| into 0139 | 0.678 | 0.685 | +0.007 | +0.001 |

**The cross-scroll gap is bias, not variance.** That averaging two independent
seeds recovers almost nothing means the two models are wrong about the same
things in the same way — which is exactly what the near-irrelevance of the seed
(mean absolute difference around 0.01) already said.

## 4b. Label efficiency — one annotated segment on the target scroll closes 82%

WARNING — **82% is a Paris4 number, and the replication says so.** The same recipe,
the same step count and the same scoring rules on **1667** (docs/18, 2026-08-31)
give **24%**: one annotated segment there lifts the five held-out segments by
+0.104 F1 against Paris4's +0.320. The *shape* replicates — the fine-tune peaks at
2,500 steps on both scrolls and declines after, which is why a step curve to 10,000
was run before saying this — but the magnitude does not. What an annotated segment
buys is scroll-dependent by a factor of three, and that variation is now the more
useful finding than the 82% itself.

LOSO-noParis4 (each seed's ckpt_020000, weights only) was fine-tuned **on w00's
labels alone**, following the official recipe unchanged for 10k steps (~27 min
per seed), then scored on **the seven segments the fine-tune never saw**. 56
cells; `runs/ink9um_scorecard/ft_paris4_matrix.csv` (plus
`ft_paris4_summary.json`).

| seg | LOSO | **FT best** | ref (training px) | **share of the gap closed** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| w01 | 0.468 | 0.868 | 0.914 | 89.6% |
| w02 | 0.418 | 0.828 | 0.916 | 82.3% |
| w03 | 0.536 | 0.830 | 0.905 | 79.7% |
| w05 | 0.397 | 0.790 | 0.858 | 85.3% |
| w06 | 0.501 | 0.807 | 0.874 | 81.9% |
| w07 | 0.555 | 0.804 | 0.868 | 79.5% |
| w09 | 0.598 | 0.826 | 0.889 | 78.3% |
| **mean** | **0.496** | **0.822** | 0.889 | **82.4%** |

- **The speed is the finding**: the step-wise mean is already at its best,
  0.822, by 2,500 steps (5k gives 0.815, 10k 0.812 — a slight decline). The
  adaptation is over in minutes, and what follows is overfitting to w00. GPU
  cost: **about 7 minutes per seed** for 2,500 steps.
- If this is what the hardest scroll gives (Paris4, margin +0.06), the others
  are likely easier.
- Seed reproducibility: ft42 and ft43 agree to within 0.01-0.02 per segment.

## The narrative these four parts support

1. **Measurement**: the cross-scroll pixel signal is +0.06 to +0.17 over the
   trivial baseline, across a three-scroll spectrum.
2. **Nature**: it is structural bias — seed-independent, immune to ensembling,
   and partly explained by representation quality (the aligned representation's
   own advantage, not family *match* — appendix 2).
3. **Cost to repair**: **one annotated segment on the target scroll plus 7 GPU
   minutes** closes about 82% of the gap (0.50 to 0.82, above the in-scroll
   honest ceiling of 0.74-0.77) — **on Paris4. On 1667 the same recipe closes
   24%** (docs/18, 2026-08-31), so quote this with the scroll attached.
4. **First Letters implication**: for an unseen scroll (0800/1447) the strategy
   is render (**in the aligned family** — because of representation quality, not
   because it matches the training corpus; appendix 2), treat direct inference
   as scouting only, annotate the single most promising segment, fine-tune, then
   re-infer everything. This document supplies the expected value of each step.

*(Caveat: the denominator of "share closed" is the reference's training-pixel
ceiling, which is a generous bar, and w00 is among the largest annotations in
Paris4. How sensitive the result is to that area — whether a smaller annotation
would do — is the obvious follow-up.)*

# Part 5: how much of that one segment has to be annotated? (2026-08-29)

Part 4 left an obvious question in its own caveat: the fine-tune that closes 82% of the
gap used **all** of w00's annotation, which is among the largest in Paris4. So how much of
it is doing the work?

`tools/make_label_budget.py` answers that by removing annotation rather than simulating
its absence. It writes label trees holding a **nested** subset of w00's annotated regions
— 25% of the annotation is a subset of the 50% — so the curve reads as "what does the next
region buy me" rather than as a comparison between unrelated annotation sets. Regions are
kept whole and near neighbours stay together, for the reason `make_validation_mask.py` does
it, and both the supervision mask and the ink labels are zeroed outside the kept regions so
no reader can pick up ink the arm is not entitled to see.

Three arms, achieved at 50.3% / 20.7% / 13.5% of the annotated area, with ink densities of
0.2284 / 0.2462 / 0.1956 against 0.2303 for the whole segment — so no arm is quietly
collecting the sparsest or richest regions. Each config differs from part 4's 100% arm in
exactly three keys (`datasets`, `out_dir`, `description`), leaving the LR schedule, the
base checkpoint and the sampler identical, and the patch count falls from 6,308 to 3,174 on
the 50.3% arm — exactly the annotation fraction. Six runs at two seeds, scored at step 2,500
(part 4's saturation point) on the same seven segments the fine-tune never saw.

| w01 | 0.468 | 0.8625 | 0.8318 | 0.7880 | 0.7395 | 92.2 / 81.1 / 68.8% |
| w02 | 0.418 | 0.8201 | 0.7826 | 0.7094 | 0.6357 | 90.7 / 72.5 / 54.1% |
| w03 | 0.536 | 0.8223 | 0.7883 | 0.7266 | 0.6824 | 88.1 / 66.6 / 51.1% |

| w06 | 0.501 | 0.7973 | 0.7667 | 0.7022 | 0.6543 | 89.7 / 67.9 / 51.7% |
| w07 | 0.555 | 0.7941 | 0.7529 | 0.6905 | 0.6517 | **82.7 / 56.7 / 40.4%** |
| w09 | 0.598 | 0.8102 | 0.7964 | 0.7678 | 0.7460 | **93.5 / 80.0 / 69.7%** |

| arm | retained | ΔF1 vs 100% | range | over the 0.03 noise floor | mean abs. seed difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50.3% | **89.3%** | **−0.0333** | −0.045 … −0.014 | 6 of 7 | 0.020 |
| 20.7% | **70.8%** | **−0.0902** | −0.111 … −0.042 | 7 of 7 | 0.034 |
| 13.5% | **55.8%** | **−0.1386** | −0.184 … −0.064 | 7 of 7 | 0.048 |

## What it says

**Half the annotation is cheap, not free.** It costs 0.033 F1 and keeps 89% of what the
full segment buys — over the noise floor on 6 of 7 segments, so the loss is real, but small
against halving the annotation work. Below that the curve bends: a fifth of the annotation
keeps 71%, an eighth keeps 56%, which throws away nearly half of what fine-tuning on one
segment was worth.

**The value of the annotation budget varies threefold by segment.** w09 is the least
sensitive (93.5 / 80.0 / 69.7) and w07 the most (82.7 / 56.7 / 40.4), and the trivial floor
does not explain it — w09's is the highest of the seven and w07's the third highest. What
makes a segment tolerant of a thin annotation is not answered here.

**Less annotation overfits sooner.** Comparing step 5,000 against 2,500, the 50.3% and
20.7% arms are flat (−0.004 and +0.001) while the 13.5% arm loses 0.027. The saturation
point part 4 found at 2,500 steps matters more, not less, as the annotation shrinks.

For the First Letters playbook this sharpens step three. "Annotate one segment" now has a
price attached: annotating half of a segment the size of w00 costs about 0.03 F1 against
annotating all of it, and going much below that is a poor trade.

Raw numbers: `runs/ink9um_scorecard/labelbudget_matrix.csv` (84 cells) and
`labelbudget_summary.json`; the arm manifest, with the region membership of each nested
subset, is `runs/ink9um_labelbudget/phercparis4-w00_label_budget.json`.

⚠️ One run (20.7%, seed 43) died at step 1,455 with a `MemoryError` inside a DataLoader
worker — the same exhaustion documented for `dataloader_workers: 12` in appendix 2 — and
the driver's retry completed it from scratch with no other change. Run times varied 38-67
minutes on an identical config depending on what else held the GPU.

# Appendix 2: the cause of the representation effect — the domain-match hypothesis, rejected (2026-08-26)

Part 3's 4-out-of-4 aligned advantage was first read as domain match. On the
villa [#1580](https://github.com/ScrollPrize/villa/pull/1580) thread Bullo27
pointed out that the public grid already contains a control for that reading
(aligned wins 0 of 4 in the ref arm, delta -0.006), and nerln split the question
off into [#1582](https://github.com/ScrollPrize/villa/issues/1582). The answer
came in two steps.

## The ref arm is a weak control

The reference is the released checkpoint, and its training contract
(`aligned21_fixed_scroll_prior.json`) is 24 aligned plus 5 native, where the
**five native ones are exactly 0139's w035, w039, w040, w041 and w044**. So the
reference did see all four compared segments in both families, which makes it a
genuine control for family exposure. But the reference is scored **on its own
training pixels**, where it has already consumed **90.7% to 96.8%** of the
available headroom (F1 0.964-0.986, leaving 0.014-0.036). There is no room for a
+0.05 gap to appear, so both readings predict the same result. **As a control it
cannot separate them.**

## So: one more arm, pre-registered

The design and the reading of each possible outcome were committed (`fb37974`)
and pushed publicly **before the arm was run**.

- **arm**: exclude w035 and w039 from **both** families, train on 25
  representations (native w040, w041 and w044 remain), seeds 42 and 43, 78,125
  steps, identical scoring — that is, **a model that has seen the family but not
  the segments**.
- native exposure: 36.3% of the 0139 patch pool, **16.4% of training batches**
  (part 3's arm: 0%).
- **why exactly two**: there are only five native representations, so the number
  excluded *is* the exposure. Excluding one leaves a single comparison pair;
  excluding four leaves only w044 native, which is effectively an aligned-only
  model again.

| seg | aligned (margin) | native (margin) | delta margin | part 3 delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| w035 | 0.791 (+0.311) | 0.757 (+0.281) | +0.030 | +0.057 |
| w039 | 0.725 (+0.240) | 0.635 (+0.153) | +0.086 | +0.066 |
| **mean** | | | **+0.058** | **+0.061** |

**Native exposure went from 0% to 16.4% and the gap moved by -0.003.** It is not
a selection-rule artifact either: max / mean / median give +0.058 / +0.055 /
+0.064, the two seeds give +0.048 and +0.058, and **each of the seven
checkpoints agrees 2 out of 2** (delta +0.050 to +0.081).

## Verdict — domain match rejected

A gap that survives regardless of family exposure is explained by **the aligned
render simply being the better input**. Aligned is a 2.399 um acquisition pooled
4x in z; native is a single 9.362 um acquisition. The amount of information that
survives into the input is not the same. (That sentence undercounts the
difference: appendix 3 measures it as 64 acquired voxels against 1.)

Every absolute score rose (the scroll-exposure effect — w035 aligned 0.740 to
0.791, native 0.679 to 0.757; w039 0.654 to 0.725 and 0.585 to 0.635). That
**only the difference between families stayed put** means scroll exposure lifted
both families symmetrically, so it cannot account for the gap.

**Reservations**: 16.4% is not balance, and because all five native
representations belong to one scroll, **a family-balanced arm cannot be built
from this corpus at all**. There are also two segments here, not four. Removing
the remaining doubt needs a native render of a different scroll, which is a data
problem rather than an analysis one.

WARNING — **trap**: both seeds die at step 5000 with a Windows DataLoader
shared-memory failure (`error 1455`, ERROR_COMMITMENT_LIMIT). Lowering
`dataloader_workers` from 12 to 6 and resuming full-state from the checkpoint
carries them to the end (~3h55m each). Recipe, seeds, step count and scoring
rules are unchanged. The worker count is an I/O parameter and does not affect
results, but state it when reproducing.

# Appendix 3: how much better, and why - the pooling, measured (2026-08-30)

Appendix 2 rejected domain match and left a one-line mechanism: aligned inputs
carry more of the scan. On [villa #1582](https://github.com/ScrollPrize/villa/issues/1582)
`nerln` made that mechanism exact - each aligned pixel is an average of many
acquired pixels while a native pixel is one - and then flagged the assumption it
rests on:

> the factor of four assumes the level-2 pyramid was built by plain averaging
> [...] I have not verified how the published pyramids were downsampled. If they
> were built by decimation rather than averaging the argument weakens.

That is checkable from the published data, so it is now checked - and
**`Bullo27` checked it first**, posting the answer on the same thread on
2026-08-30 before this was written up. Their result and this one agree, and
theirs was the tighter measurement; what follows says so and then adds the part
that is still missing from the thread.

## What was measured

`tools/check_pyramid_pooling.py` reads small windows straight out of the
published surface volumes over anonymous https and compares each pyramid level
against the level below it: as a 2x2 **mean**, and as a 2x2 **decimation** (keep
one pixel of four). Three scrolls, both level transitions, three windows of 64x64
coarse pixels at three z planes each - 18 window comparisons.

| source segment | levels | max abs diff, round-half-up mean | exact | max abs diff, unrounded mean | max abs diff, decimation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| phercparis4-w01 | 0 to 1 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 16 |
| phercparis4-w01 | 1 to 2 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 43 |
| pherc1667-w013 | 0 to 1 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 26 |
| pherc1667-w013 | 1 to 2 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 54 |
| pherc0139-w035 | 0 to 1 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 17 |
| pherc0139-w035 | 1 to 2 | **0** | 100% | 0.50 | 43 |

**Every level is a 2x2 mean, byte-exact.** Model the per-level rounding as
round-half-up and the coarse level reproduces to the last grey level in all 18
windows. Decimation is off by 16 to 98.

WARNING - **the first version of this appendix reported 0.50 and "~87% exact",
and that was a defect in the measurement, not in the pyramid.** It compared
against the *unrounded* mean, so the residual it saw was the rounding it had not
modelled, and its "exact" column used numpy's round-half-to-even. Bullo27's
comment made the right model obvious; the tool now applies round-half-up, reports
the unrounded residual alongside, and gets 0.

**The volumes also say so themselves.** `multiscales[0].metadata` carries
`downsampling_method: "mean"` - which this tool did not read until Bullo27's
comment pointed at it, and now does. A declaration and a measurement are
different kinds of evidence; the volumes carry both and they agree.

**And the pyramid is XY-only**, which the volumes' own OME metadata states and
this run records: the scale goes `[2.4, 2.4, 2.4]` to `[2.4, 4.8, 4.8]` to
`[2.4, 9.6, 9.6]`. The z axis is never downsampled.

## The count that follows

`scripts/prepare_9um_isotropic_input.py` builds an aligned input by reading
**level 2** and then mean-pooling **4 z planes** (`POOL_Z = 4`, and those planes
are still 2.399 um apart because the pyramid never touched z). So:

- one level-2 pixel = mean of a 4x4 block of acquired pixels = **16 samples**;
- one output voxel = mean of 4 such planes = **64 acquired 2.399 um voxels**,
  spanning 9.596 um on each axis;
- a native voxel covering the same space is **one** 9.362 um acquisition.

nerln's factor of 16 is the in-plane half of it. The z pool multiplies it a
second time, independently, precisely because the pyramid is XY-only.

## What this does and does not license

It settles the assumption: the averaging is real, so "more of the scan survives
into the input" is a statement about 64 samples against 1, not a figure of
speech. It does **not** license a numerical SNR claim - that would need the
acquisition noise to be independent between neighbouring 2.399 um voxels, which
is not measured here. What is measured is that the field being averaged is not
flat: across the twelve windows, the standard deviation inside the 2x2 blocks
each level averages runs 2.0 to 5.8 grey levels, so the pooling is doing work
rather than reproducing a constant.

It also sits consistently with the negative result in
[docs/18](18_uda_design.md): if the advantage is sample count, no filter applied
to a single-sample input can restore it, and arm A found that matching the
radial power spectrum recovers no F1 at all. A spectrum can be reshaped;
measurements that were never taken cannot be.

Raw reports: `runs/pyramid/*_pooling.json` (three segments, 18 windows).

**Refined 2026-08-31, and it matters for anyone acting on this.** The 64-voxel averaging above
is a fact of construction, and this document was careful not to turn it into an SNR ratio. A
later measurement ([docs/21](21_snr_augmentation.md)) shows why that caution was right: after
the trainer's own normalisation, the native input carries *less* high-frequency energy than
the aligned one, on all four paired segments and in 24 of 24 cells across two normalisations
and three scales. So the difference the model sees is that native is **smoother**, not
noisier. "Aligned wins because it has less noise" is the wrong shape of explanation, and an
intervention built on it -- adding noise during training so the model tolerates native input
-- was stopped by its own calibration before it consumed a GPU hour.
